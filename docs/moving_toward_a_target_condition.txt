Moving Toward a Target Condition
(an original summary of the idea, not a reproduction of any book - see attribution at the end)

THE PATH IS NEVER FULLY VISIBLE

Once a target condition exists, the honest starting position is that you don't yet know exactly
how you'll reach it. That's not a planning failure - it's just true, the same way a pilot can't
chart every gust of wind between cruising altitude and the runway before taking off. Whatever plan
gets made will be wrong in some of its details the moment reality pushes back on it. The useful
move isn't a better plan up front; it's shorter feedback loops once you're moving.

EXPERIMENT, DON'T DEBATE

Hypotheses get tested by actually doing something and checking what happened - not by discussing
what probably would happen (Rother's own book has a name for this: "testing over talking"). This
sounds obvious and gets violated constantly, usually dressed up
as "research." A single query to a language model, phrased casually, answered without any real
verification, isn't testing - it's a guess with better formatting. That happened directly in this
project: an early orientation step asked one AI system a single low-effort question and treated the
answer as fact, including a claimed community that turned out not to check out on closer look.
Real testing meant going back, verifying every concrete claim independently, and pushing a second,
harder pass that actively looked for what the first answer got wrong - which is how the mistake
actually got found.

GO AND SEE

Secondhand information, however confident it sounds, is not a substitute for looking at the actual
thing yourself. A model's opinion about which online communities are receptive is not the same as
reading that community's own posted rules. In this project, exactly that gap mattered: a rule
banning links to certain large platforms - discovered only by reading the actual rules directly -
would have been missed by any amount of secondhand research, because it was specific, current, and
non-obvious. "Go and see" doesn't mean distrust everything; it means treat a claim as unverified
until you or someone trustworthy has actually looked.

DON'T WAIT FOR THE PERFECT STEP

Once you know roughly what's wrong, the instinct is often to fix it properly before doing anything
else - redesign the thing, polish the pitch, build the better version, THEN act. Resist that. A
rough, provisional step taken now teaches you more than a polished one taken later, because it
surfaces the next real obstacle immediately instead of after a delay spent perfecting something
whose flaws might not even have been the actual problem. A book with an admittedly rushed cover and
title still goes out as-is on day one, not after a redesign - because the untested variable was
never the design, it was whether anyone outside a small circle had ever actually seen it.

RAPID, SMALL, CHECKED CYCLES

Long stretches between checking in on progress produce very little real learning, because by the
time you look, the specific cause of whatever went wrong is gone, mixed in with everything else
that happened in between. Short cycles - do one small thing, check it soon, adjust - keep the
cause and effect close enough together to actually see clearly. This is also why a target condition
with a very long deadline is usually a mistake: it removes the pressure to check in frequently,
which is where almost all the real information comes from.

A WAY TO KEEP MOVING WHEN STUCK

A short set of questions, credited to Rother's own formulation, works as a fast way to re-orient at
any point in a cycle: What's the target condition? What's actually true right now? What's the one
obstacle being worked on at this moment - not all of them, just the one in front of you? What's the
next concrete step? And when will you actually check what that step taught you? Answering these in
order, honestly, is usually enough to unstick a cycle that's drifted into planning instead of
moving.

WHAT COUNTS AS A GOOD RESULT

A prediction that turns out wrong is not a failed effort - it's the more informative outcome of the
two, because it means something was actually learned rather than just confirmed. A stated
prediction of "nothing will happen" that turns out true is a real, useful, honestly-earned fact
after however long the thing sat untested. The same prediction turning out false is even more
useful, because it means the model of the situation was incomplete in a way now worth
understanding. Either way beats never having made a falsifiable prediction at all.

moving toward a target condition - comes from Mike Rother's book "Toyota Kata" (2009). This
